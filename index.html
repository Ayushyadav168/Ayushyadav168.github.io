About

Engineer first, risk analyst by choice

I came into finance from computer science, and that order matters. I learned to build systems before I learned to value businesses, so I tend to ask what breaks a model before I ask what it is worth.

At NIT Kurukshetra I read probability, statistics, linear algebra and numerical methods alongside data structures — and spent the spare hours shipping two products rather than only studying. SharePicker, an equity screener, and FinSight, a personal finance and portfolio app, reached about 2,500 users between them. Writing the data pipelines behind them is why I now trust a number only once I know how it was assembled.

The finance side started with equity research — 12+ institutional-style reports through the IIM Bangalore Associate Programme, each one forced through the same seven steps and each one ending with a view I had to defend. It continued in an investment banking division, where the models stopped being an exercise: three-statement forecasts, DCF and comps on live mandates, with real diligence underneath them — revenue quality, working capital, related-party flows, promoter pledges.

Somewhere in that, the risk half became the part I actually enjoy. FRM Part I (GARP) is cleared and Part II is in preparation, because the question I keep returning to is not what a business is worth on the base case, but what it is worth when two assumptions go wrong at once. A valuation without that is a guess with decimal places.

Outside the desk I am building two things of my own: Safe Horizon, a risk advisory practice for small and growing enterprises, and an SME consulting LLP with my brother. Both exist because the companies that most need an honest risk opinion are the ones least likely to be sold one.

The long view

To build India's most trusted independent risk advisory firm — the one people call when they want the real answer, not the comfortable one.

Case study

A funnel dashboard, and the seven things wrong with the data under it

A lending lead-funnel build in Power BI. The dashboard was the deliverable; the data quality work was the job.

Customer Direct lead funnel — Power BI

19,468 leads · 68,964 call logs · 7 tables · 15 KPIs across 5 comparison columns

What the raw data did wrong

  • 76 dupesExact duplicate lead rows broke the 1:many relationship to call logs. De-duplicated on lead_id.
  • 1,012 rowsInbound calls mixed in with outbound dials. Inbound connect 100% of the time, so they inflated connectivity. Filtered out.
  • 45 orphansCalls referencing lead_ids absent from the leads sheet. Inner-joined.
  • "0"Used as the placeholder for "no UTM" on 7,600+ non-paid leads — it reads as a campaign named zero and sorts to the top of every slicer. Mapped to "(none)".
  • case drift"GoogleAds" and "google", "CPC" and "cpc" — one real source appearing twice in every slicer. Normalised.
  • 894 leadsAssigned to a "demo agent". Flagged rather than deleted: the target lead count includes them.
  • date colAd spend dates arrived in whatever format the platform exported. Parsed defensively across four layouts so a re-export cannot silently break the relationship.

All fixes sit in Power Query, so the model reads the original workbook untouched and re-cleans on every refresh. The same rules are mirrored in SQL.

The false positive I did not ship

A naive comparison flags 997 leads as qualified before they were created. They were not. qualified_date is date-only and stored at midnight; created_at is a full timestamp, so a lead created at 19:46 and qualified the same day tests as earlier. Compared at day level the genuine count is zero — there is no ordering fault in the funnel.

It is in the note because it is the kind of finding that costs a reviewer's trust if it ships.

Insight 1 — cheap leads, cheaper disbursals

Meta delivers leads at ₹254 against Google's ₹547, but only 5.0% of Meta leads qualify versus 12.2% for Google. The instinct is to move budget to Google. Carried to the end of the funnel it reverses: cost per disbursal is ₹55,378 on Meta against ₹75,007 on Google. Meta was being judged at the top of the funnel, which is exactly where it looks worst.

Insight 2 — the miss was operational

Outbound dials run 600–930 a day, then fall to 214 and 243 on two consecutive days before recovering to 829 — a capacity outage against a weekday median of 642. That one event drives Leads Attempted down 5.7% and Total Attempts down 6.8% week on week, while lead supply was flat at +0.2% and connectivity improved. The red cells belonged to the dialler owner, not to marketing.

Notes

What I write about

Short pieces from the work itself — a number that behaved unexpectedly, a signal worth checking, a conclusion that reversed when carried one step further.

Funnel economics

Cheap leads are not cheap disbursals

A lending channel delivering leads at ₹254 looked low-intent next to one at ₹547 — qualification of 5.0% against 12.2%. Budget was about to move. Carried to disbursal, cost per funded loan was ₹55,378 against ₹75,007, and the ranking flipped.

The channel was being judged at the stage where it looks worst. Pick the metric at the end of the funnel, not the top.

Accrual quality

When profit arrives faster than cash

The OCF-versus-PAT gap is the first thing I check on a growth story. Profit can be reported on revenue that has not been collected, and the gap widens quietly for several quarters before receivable days make it obvious.

A company generating more cash than it reports as profit is the healthy version of this. The reverse deserves a sentence in every report.

Valuation

A 14% gap hiding in the peer set

Peer multiples are rarely comparable as published. Normalising the peer set and bridging margin differences moved implied value on a target by roughly 14% — enough to change the pricing conversation rather than confirm it.

A ratio means nothing in isolation. Compare against the company's own history first, the peer group second.

Data discipline

The finding I deleted before shipping

997 records appeared to be qualified before they existed. The cause was a date-only column stored at midnight against a full timestamp — compared at day level, the genuine count was zero.

A false positive that reaches a reviewer costs more trust than a missed one. Test the data model before you report the anomaly.
